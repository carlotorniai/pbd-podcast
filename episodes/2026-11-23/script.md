# Pharma and Biotech Daily, 2026-11-23

Good morning from Pharma and Biotech Daily, the briefing on what matters in pharma and biotech. It's Monday, November 23.

Since Friday, Merck and Kelun-Biotech have reported a survival win for their TROP2 antibody drug conjugate in advanced stomach and esophageal cancer, and the FDA has turned down Capricor's cell therapy for Duchenne muscular dystrophy for a second time, after a long and contested review.

Our lead story comes from BioSpace. Merck and its Chinese partner Kelun-Biotech said sacituzumab tirumotecan, known as sac T M T, met the main goal of the Phase three TroFuse-015 study. It enrolled four hundred seventy three patients with advanced gastroesophageal adenocarcinoma who had already received at least two lines of therapy, and improved overall survival compared with the doctor's choice of treatment. It missed its secondary goals on progression free survival and response rate, and the companies are holding the numbers for a medical meeting. Merck calls this the first TROP2 drug to extend survival in a global Phase three trial in this cancer, and it is the second late stage win in an eighteen study program that Merck is counting on as Keytruda approaches loss of exclusivity at the end of twenty twenty eight.

Next, a setback for a rare disease biotech. BioSpace reports that the FDA has issued a second complete response letter for deramiocel, Capricor Therapeutics' cell therapy for Duchenne muscular dystrophy. Capricor first sought approval for the heart muscle disease linked to Duchenne, then shifted its case toward upper limb function after an advisory panel voted nine to three against the drug in July. The agency said the data lacked substantial evidence of effectiveness. The FDA has argued that the Phase three HOPE-3 study missed its prespecified endpoints, a reading Capricor disputes. Shares fell nearly eighty percent after hours on Friday, and the company is reviewing the letter. The case was seen as an early test of the agency's fit for purpose framework for rare diseases, and it shows the limits of that flexibility when a pivotal trial is contested.

Our third story is an oncology update, also from BioSpace. Revolution Medicines presented new data on Rasonque, its RAS inhibitor daraxonrasib, given with chemotherapy as a first treatment for metastatic pancreatic cancer. Among sixty one patients in an early stage study, sixty point seven percent responded, and about eight percent had a complete response. Median progression free survival reached twelve point eight months, and seventy six point six percent of patients were alive at one year. The results were shown at the EORTC, NCI and AACR meeting in Spain. Rasonque won FDA approval in August for previously treated disease, so a move into first line would greatly widen its reach. The Phase three RASolute 303 trial will settle that question, and analysts at Truist expect a readout around twenty twenty eight.

Finally, a delay at the agency. Savara said on Friday that the FDA will not meet the November twenty second decision date for molgramostim, an inhaled treatment for autoimmune pulmonary alveolar proteinosis, a rare lung disease. Savara plans to sell the drug as Molbreevi. The deadline had already been pushed back by three months in April, and the agency has not set a new one. The Phase three IMPALA-2 trial met its primary goal on a lung function measure at twenty four weeks. Missed decision dates are rare, and analysts at Jefferies said this one may reflect strained capacity at the FDA, while keeping high confidence in approval and estimating peak sales above five hundred million dollars. If cleared, it would be the first approved therapy for the disease, according to BioSpace.

The thread running through today's four stories is the FDA itself: a rejection, a missed deadline and a survival result headed to regulators all show that the agency's evidence standards and pace now move biotech value as much as the clinical data do.
