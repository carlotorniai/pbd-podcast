# Pharma and Biotech Daily, 2026-11-19

Good morning from Pharma and Biotech Daily, the briefing on what matters in pharma and biotech. It's Thursday, November 19.

Today, Pfizer makes its first move into in vivo CAR T with a Chinese partner, and Merck shuts down an entire blood cancer program after a Phase three safety review.

First, Pfizer. According to BioSpace, the company has licensed three autoimmune CAR T programs from Starna Therapeutics, a Chinese company that builds RNA medicines delivered through targeted lipid nanoparticles. Pfizer pays three hundred thirty million dollars upfront, with up to two point three billion more in milestones plus royalties, for a total of up to two point six billion. All three programs are preclinical, and Pfizer also gains rights to use the platform in immunology and in oncology. This is the company's first in vivo cell therapy work, after earlier ex vivo efforts in cancer. Why it matters: making CAR T cells inside the body could remove the cell harvesting and manufacturing that keep these therapies out of most autoimmune clinics, and big pharma keeps sourcing that innovation from China.

Next, Merck. The company said on Tuesday afternoon it is ending development of nemtabrutinib, its oral non covalent BTK inhibitor, across the whole BELLWAVE program. An external data monitoring committee reviewed the Phase three BELLWAVE eleven study in chronic lymphocytic leukemia and signaled that the benefits may not outweigh the risks. Merck's own review of the data agreed, so the BELLWAVE eight and BELLWAVE ten Phase three trials stop as well. Fierce Biotech framed it as the end of a two point seven billion dollar program. For the market, the decision removes a would be challenger to the established BTK drugs in leukemia, and it is a reminder that a late stage asset can fall away on a single interim look.

An update on the story we covered on Monday. The FDA has published the rejection letter for Summit Therapeutics and Akeso's ivonescimab in EGFR mutated lung cancer, and it goes beyond the overall survival miss. Fierce Pharma reports that ninety three percent of progression free survival events came from patients enrolled in China, and the agency said it could not tell whether the results apply to American patients. Median progression free survival rose from four point four to six point eight months, a gain the FDA called modest against added toxicity. It wants a new global trial. BioPharma Dive quotes a Leerink analyst saying a fresh application alone is unlikely to change the outcome. The lesson for sponsors: trials built mostly in one country face hard questions in Washington.

Turning to Alzheimer's. At the CTAD meeting in Boston on Wednesday, Bristol Myers Squibb presented data on Cobenfy, xanomeline and trospium, in psychosis linked to Alzheimer's disease. BioSpace reports that in ADEPT one, two hundred seventy three patients who completed a twelve week open label phase showed fewer hallucinations and delusions, though the relapse results and placebo comparisons were held back. Treatment related adverse events reached about fifty seven percent in that study and sixty five percent in the year long ADEPT three extension, which caught analysts' attention. Bristol has also moved to three times daily dosing. The randomized ADEPT two readout is now expected in twenty twenty seven, so the commercial case in this population stays open for another year.

Finally, a neuroscience deal. BioPharma Dive reports that Biogen is paying Trimtech Therapeutics more than thirty two million dollars upfront to develop small molecule protein degraders for neurodegenerative disease. The molecules are designed to cross into the central nervous system and clear toxic protein buildups. Biogen holds an option to license the resulting drugs exclusively and would then fund development and sales, while Trimtech could earn up to four hundred eleven million dollars in milestones plus royalties. The structure keeps Biogen's upfront exposure small while it buys access to a modality that has mostly been tested in cancer, and it shows the company rebuilding its brain pipeline through partnerships.

The common thread today is risk being priced in public: in early platforms bought with options, in late stage programs cut on safety, and in regulators asking where the data came from.
