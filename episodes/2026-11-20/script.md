# Pharma and Biotech Daily, 2026-11-20

Good morning from Pharma and Biotech Daily, the briefing on what matters in pharma and biotech. It's Friday, November 20.

Today, Regeneron wins approval for its first RNA interference drug and a federal commission warns that China could come to dominate biotech, and we also cover a reported Sanofi interest in Insmed and a GLP one licensing deal aimed at emerging markets.

Our lead comes from BioPharma Dive. On Wednesday the FDA approved Regeneron's Livsayis, or cemdisiran, for adults with generalized myasthenia gravis who carry antibodies against the acetylcholine receptor. The drug shuts down production of the complement protein C5 and is injected under the skin four times a year. Alnylam discovered it and licensed it to Regeneron in 2019, and now collects a twenty five million dollar milestone plus royalties. Regeneron set a list price of four hundred ten thousand dollars a year. This is the first RNA interference therapy cleared for an autoimmune disease, and it enters a crowded market where argenx and AstraZeneca already compete. A TD Cowen analyst sees peak sales of one point five billion dollars, while RBC expects a measured start in a tough launch environment.

Next, a policy story with long reach. BioPharma Dive reports that the National Security Commission on Emerging Biotechnology, an eleven member panel created by Congress, released a report on Wednesday warning that China's new five year plan could let it corner the biopharmaceutical sector. Beijing aims to originate a quarter of the world's new drugs, and the commission sees a risk that supply chains could be turned against American patients. It backs a Treasury proposal to bring biotech under the COINS Act, which could restrict certain transactions with Chinese companies. Business development teams should pay close attention here. BioPharma Dive counts more than one hundred twenty licensing deals between American or European companies and Chinese developers since the start of 2025, many of them for early, preclinical assets.

On the deal front, Fierce Pharma and several other outlets, citing Bloomberg, report that Sanofi is weighing a takeover of Insmed, the maker of the lung drug Brinsupri, also called brensocatib. Nothing has been announced by either company so far. The strategic logic is easy to follow. Sanofi's biggest product, Dupixent, faces a patent cliff, and Fierce describes Brinsupri as a blockbuster in the making. Insmed would be a very expensive target, and Scrip has already asked whether Sanofi can afford it. If a bid does arrive, reports suggest it could be the largest biotech acquisition in years, and a clear sign of how hard big pharma is working to replace revenue before key patents expire. For now, treat it as a report and watch for a formal statement.

Last, a licensing deal shows how Chinese metabolic drugs are finding routes abroad. In a joint press release, Abu Dhabi based Arcera Life Sciences has taken exclusive rights to Innogen Pharmaceuticals' efsubaglutide alfa, a GLP one receptor agonist approved for type two diabetes in mainland China. Arcera will handle approvals and sales in selected markets across the Middle East, Africa, Eastern Europe and Latin America. Innogen receives an upfront payment, milestones and tiered royalties. The release gives no figures, though Fierce Pharma puts the deal above one billion dollars. The drug is dosed weekly or every two weeks, and in an eighteen week study in China it produced average weight loss of about ten percent. Emerging markets are now a serious channel for GLP one medicines beyond the established brands.

The common thread today is the race for the next source of growth, whether that means a new drug modality, a new territory, a large acquisition or tighter control over where the next wave of innovation comes from, and that race is now shaped as much by policy in Washington and Beijing as by science.
