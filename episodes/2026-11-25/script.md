# Pharma and Biotech Daily, 2026-11-25

Good morning from Pharma and Biotech Daily, the briefing on what matters in pharma and biotech. It's Wednesday, November 25.

Today, Sanofi agrees to pay about forty one billion dollars for Insmed, and Medicare publishes its third round of negotiated drug prices, the first to reach physician administered medicines.

An update on the story we covered on Friday. Sanofi has signed a definitive agreement to acquire Insmed for one hundred seventy five dollars per share in cash, an equity value of roughly forty one billion dollars, according to the company's announcement filed with the SEC. Sanofi picks up Brinsupri, brensocatib, the once daily oral pill for non cystic fibrosis bronchiectasis that brought in about one point one billion dollars through September, plus Arikayce for a hard to treat lung infection, and the Phase three inhaled candidate treprostinil palmitil in pulmonary hypertension and lung fibrosis. Financing comes from debt and cash, and closing is expected in the first half of twenty twenty seven. STAT calls it one of the largest pharma takeovers in years. The logic is clear: Sanofi needs new growth before Dupixent loses protection.

Next, drug pricing. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services announced negotiated prices for thirteen drugs in the third cycle of the program created by the Inflation Reduction Act. CMS puts the savings at nine point two billion dollars, or forty five percent, with the new prices starting in January twenty twenty eight. For the first time the list includes Part B drugs given in the clinic, alongside retail Part D medicines, and Tradjenta becomes the first drug to be renegotiated. STAT notes that once existing manufacturer discounts are counted, the real reduction is closer to five and a half billion dollars. For companies with physician administered biologics, price negotiation is now a direct planning risk.

Turning to approvals, the FDA has cleared Cogent Biosciences' Ekitna, bezuclastinib, taken with sunitinib for adults with advanced gastrointestinal stromal tumors previously treated with imatinib. Cogent says it is the first new option in this setting in more than twenty years. In the Phase three PEAK trial, the combination cut the risk of progression or death by half, with median progression free survival of sixteen and a half months against just over nine months on sunitinib alone. Response rates were forty six percent versus twenty six percent. The label carries a boxed warning for liver toxicity. The drug is available now, and it gives Cogent its first commercial product, with a second indication in systemic mastocytosis in development.

Another first came from BridgeBio. The FDA approved Welgemda, ribitol, for limb girdle muscular dystrophy type two I, also called R nine, a rare inherited muscle disease caused by a faulty FKRP enzyme. BioPharma Dive reports it is the first approved treatment for the condition. In a placebo controlled Phase three study, the twice daily oral solution improved a biochemical marker, a timed walking test and a breathing measure at twelve months, and diarrhea was the most common side effect. BridgeBio set an annual list price of nine hundred eighty three thousand five hundred dollars and has identified about five hundred confirmed patients in the United States so far. The price shows how rare disease launches are still being valued.

Finally, a manufacturing deal. Merck KGaA, the German group known in North America as MilliporeSigma, has started its tender offer for Rapid Micro Biosystems at seventy five cents per share in cash, according to a company press release. Rapid makes the Growth Direct system, which automates microbial quality control testing in drug and biologics plants. Rapid's board backs the offer, holders of about a fifth of its main share class have agreed to tender, and the offer runs until December eighth. The move follows Merck KGaA's plan to sell its contract manufacturing unit to Lonza, and it signals a sharper focus on tools that make quality testing faster.

The common thread today is pressure on the price of a medicine, from Washington's negotiators to the premiums buyers will pay for proven revenue.
