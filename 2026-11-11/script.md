# Pharma and Biotech Daily, 2026-11-11

Good morning from Pharma and Biotech Daily, the briefing on what matters in pharma and biotech. It's Wednesday, November 11.

Base editing has its first positive late stage readout, and the FDA has approved a new oral pill for a rare joint tumor.

We start with Beam Therapeutics. BioPharma Dive reports that Beam's sickle cell therapy, ristoglogene autogetemcel, known as risto-cel, met its main goal in the pivotal BEACON study: freedom from severe vaso-occlusive crises. The analysis covers thirty one patients with twelve months of follow-up. Beam has not released the numbers and plans to file with the FDA as soon as year end. The therapy edits a patient's own cells to switch fetal hemoglobin back on. This matters because Vertex and CRISPR Therapeutics' Casgevy has grown slowly, with about two hundred nineteen million dollars in sales in the first nine months of the year. Analysts at RBC say a shorter manufacturing process could help risto-cel compete. Beam shares still fell as much as ten percent on Tuesday.

Next, a regulatory decision. The FDA has approved Pimjetmi, pimicotinib, for adults with symptomatic tenosynovial giant cell tumor when surgery could worsen function or cause serious harm. The drug was developed by Shanghai based Abbisko Therapeutics and is licensed globally to Merck KGaA of Darmstadt, Germany. In the Phase three MANEUVER trial, the company says, showed a response rate of fifty six percent at week twenty five, against three percent on placebo, with gains in range of motion, physical function and pain. China, Canada and Switzerland approved it first. For Merck KGaA, this is a new United States launch. For Abbisko, it brings a milestone payment on first sales plus double digit royalties, a clear example of a Chinese discovered drug reaching American patients through a Western partner.

That point leads into policy. The Biotechnology Innovation Organization, the main industry lobby, released a plan called BioAdvance, with seven priorities for Congress and the administration. BioPharma Dive reports the plan asks for more flexible rules on first in human trials, centralized approvals across trial sites, steadier funding for the National Institutes of Health, and a White House Office of Biotechnology. The backdrop is China, which BIO says now starts over a quarter of global clinical trials. BioPharma Dive counts more than one hundred licensing deals between Western companies and Chinese drugmakers. The Treasury has proposed bringing biotech under the COINS Act, which could add scrutiny to those ties, and BIO has criticized that approach. The plan shows where lobbying will push next: faster early stage trials at home.

Turning to a smaller company with a big filing. Gossamer Bio reported that it submitted its first new drug application in September, for seralutinib in pulmonary arterial hypertension, and is preparing for a possible launch in twenty twenty seven. The filing rests on the Phase three PROSERA study, supported by the earlier TORREY trial. Gossamer took back worldwide rights from Chiesi in July. Cash stood at about fifty one million dollars at the end of September, and a private placement could add up to about one hundred twenty five million dollars if the FDA accepts the application. The acceptance decision is now the key event for the company and for treatment options in the disease.

Finally, another gene editing milestone. Metagenomi said the FDA cleared its investigational new drug application for MGX-001 in hemophilia A. The one time treatment pairs a viral vector carrying the factor eight gene with a lipid nanoparticle carrying its own CRISPR enzyme. The goal is to insert the gene next to the albumin gene so the liver produces factor eight steadily. In monkeys, factor eight activity reached the normal range. The Phase one and two INTEGR8 trial should start in the first half of twenty twenty seven, with first data in the second half. Metagenomi says its cash of about one hundred two million dollars funds operations into twenty twenty eight. More preclinical data will be shown at the American Society of Hematology meeting in December.

The thread today is genetic medicine and global competition: new editing tools are maturing in the clinic just as the industry debates where the next wave of drugs will be discovered and tested.
